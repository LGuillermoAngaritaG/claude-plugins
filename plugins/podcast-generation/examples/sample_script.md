**HOST_A:** Welcome in. Today is all about making a really good cup of coffee at home, and we will keep it to three things: the beans, the grind, and the water.
**HOST_B:** Three things, and those three cover most of what separates a flat cup from a great one. You do not need an expensive machine. You need fresh beans, the right grind, and water at the right heat.
**HOST_A:** Start with the beans, then. What does fresh actually mean?
**HOST_B:** Fresh means roasted recently, ideally within the last two to four weeks. Roasted coffee slowly loses the gases and oils that carry its aroma. So look for a roast date on the bag, not just a best before date. Buy whole beans when you can, because ground coffee goes stale within days.
**HOST_A:** And once they are home, where do they live?
**HOST_B:** Somewhere dark, cool and dry, in a container that closes tight. Not the fridge, because the beans soak up moisture and every smell in there. Buy what you will finish in a couple of weeks, and half the problem is solved.
**HOST_A:** So, fresh beans, stored well. Now let's talk about the grind, because I suspect that is where most of us go wrong.
**HOST_B:** It often is. The grind controls how quickly water pulls flavour out of the coffee. Fine grounds give up their flavour fast, and coarse grounds give it up slowly. The trick is matching the grind to how long the water and the coffee spend together.
**HOST_A:** So a quick brew wants a finer grind, and a slow one
**HOST_B:** wants a coarser grind, exactly. A French press steeps for about four minutes, so it likes coarse grounds, like rough sea salt. A pour over takes around three minutes and likes something closer to table salt. Espresso is over in under half a minute, so it needs a very fine powder.
**HOST_A:** How do I know if I got it wrong?
**HOST_B:** Your tongue tells you. If the cup is sour and thin, the water did not pull out enough, so grind finer next time. If it is bitter and harsh, it pulled out too much, so grind coarser. Change one thing at a time, and within a few mornings you will find your setting. A burr grinder helps here, because it makes even pieces, while a blade grinder makes dust and chunks.
**HOST_A:** That leads us to the last piece, the water.
**HOST_B:** Water is almost all of the cup, so it matters more than people think. Use water that tastes good on its own. If your tap water tastes of chlorine, a simple filter makes a real difference. For temperature, aim for just off the boil, somewhere between ninety and ninety six degrees Celsius. The easy way is to boil the kettle and let it rest for about thirty seconds.
**HOST_A:** And how much coffee for how much water?
**HOST_B:** A good starting point is one part coffee to about sixteen parts water, by weight. In practice that is about fifteen grams of coffee to a quarter litre of water, one generous mug. A small kitchen scale makes this repeatable, and repeatable is what lets you improve.
**HOST_A:** So, to sum up. Fresh whole beans, stored somewhere dark and dry. A grind that matches your brewing time, adjusted by taste. And good water, just off the boil, at about one to sixteen.
**HOST_B:** That is the whole recipe. Get those three right, and everything after that is personal taste, which is the fun part.
**HOST_A:** Thanks for listening, and enjoy your next cup.
