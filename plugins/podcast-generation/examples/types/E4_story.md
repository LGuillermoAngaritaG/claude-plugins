**HOST_A:** London, a bright Saturday in June two thousand. A new footbridge of steel has just opened over the Thames, and thousands of people are walking across it. Then, under their feet, the bridge begins to move. The events here are real; the words people say are imagined.
**HOST_A:** This is a story about resonance, in three steps: a rhythm, a crowd that falls into it, and a fix.
**HOST_A:** First, the rhythm. Every bridge can sway, and every bridge has a pace at which it likes to sway, like a swing that always goes to and fro at the same speed. For this bridge, a gentle sideways sway was easy.
**HOST_C:** Is it meant to do that?
**HOST_A:** A woman holds the rail. The bridge is moving a few centimetres from side to side, enough to make people stop smiling.
**HOST_A:** Second, the crowd. When the floor moves under you, you widen your step and lean with it, without thinking. Thousands of people did that at once, and their steps fell into the bridge's rhythm. Each step pushed the bridge a little further, which made people lean more. A small sway fed itself. Engineers later gave it a name: synchronous lateral excitation. Londoners gave it a better one: the Wobbly Bridge.
**HOST_B:** We close it. Today.
**HOST_A:** An engineer says it, or something like it. The bridge was shut two days after it opened.
**HOST_A:** Third, the fix. The engineers could have made the bridge stiffer, but that would have changed how it looked. Instead they fitted dampers, parts that soak up motion the way a shock absorber does in a car. In February two thousand two, the bridge opened again, and it has stayed still since.
**HOST_A:** So: everything that can move has a rhythm, a crowd can fall into it and feed it, and engineers tame it by soaking up the motion. The next time you cross a bridge, feel for its rhythm. Goodbye for now.
